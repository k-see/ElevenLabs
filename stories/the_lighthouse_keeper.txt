The Last Light on Corran Point

For forty-one years, Edda Marsh had climbed the hundred and twelve steps of the Corran Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She knew each one by its sound. The seventh step groaned. The fifty-third rang faintly, like a spoon against a cup. And the last step, the one just before the lamp room, said nothing at all, as if it were holding its breath.

Tonight was supposed to be her final climb. The letter from the maritime office sat folded on her kitchen table: the light would be automated by Monday. "A sensor and a timer," the young man on the telephone had said, kindly enough. "It won't even need you to wind it."

Edda reached the lamp room and stood a while, watching the sea go from pewter to ink. Then she saw it. Far out, beyond the shoals, a small light was blinking. Not steady, like a ship's lantern. Three short. Three long. Three short.

She frowned. Her hands knew what to do before her head did. She lit the great lamp, and as the beam swept outward she grabbed the old signal shutter from its hook on the wall, the one nobody had used since the radios came, and she answered. I see you. Hold on.

Then she picked up the telephone and called the lifeboat station.

It was a fishing skiff, they told her later, its engine dead and its radio drowned. A father and his twelve-year-old daughter. The girl had remembered a lesson from a school trip to the lighthouse, years ago. A woman with silver hair had shown the class how sailors once spoke with light.

On Monday the men came with their sensor and their timer. Edda made them tea and showed them where everything was. She did not argue.

But on Tuesday evening, as the sun went down, the girl from the skiff knocked on her door holding a flashlight and a notebook.

"Will you teach me?" she asked.

And Edda Marsh, who had thought her last climb was behind her, found that she still had a hundred and twelve steps left in her after all.
